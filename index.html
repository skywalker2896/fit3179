Beating the Odds at Australian Open Does ranking predicts who wins? From draw to the final point

The Australian Open is the first of the four Grand Slams, held every January in Melbourne Park. Starting with qualifying and finishing with the men's and women's finals, it spans about three weeks and draws record crowds, making it one of the most attended sports events in Australia.

Each year, 256 players enter the 2 singles main draw, 32 seeded players each according to their world ranking. In theory, seeding protects the favourites from leaving early but in practice, upsets happen and that tension to whether ranking predicts who wins.

Section 3 · Does Ranking Predict Performance

Figure 05 · Heatmap Matrix

Players Expected vs Actual Exit Round

Source: Tennis My Life

How to read this

Each player is placed in the column for the round their seed predicts they will reach (seeds 1–4: SF, 5–8: QF, 9–16: R16, 17–32: R32, unseeded: R1/R2) and the row for the round they actually exited.

Seeding usually sets expectation. In the 2024 men’s draw, 87 unseeded players exited in the first two rounds as expected, and most seeded players finished close to their predicted round. Off-diagonal cells show the exceptions, such as lower seeds falling early or outsiders going deep.

Higher-ranked players generally spend more time on court,

they win more rounds and therefore play more matches. Seeds 1 and 3 are at the top, at around 245 games each, and totals fall as seed number rises. Figure 07 · Dumbbell Plot

Ranking Gap vs Who Wins by Round 2026

Source: Kaggle

Every match is a test of expectation, the crowd are more likely to support highly ranked favourites than unseeded players. In the round of 16, the favourites mostly delivered: seven of the eight higher-ranked players won. The one upset, Learner Tien (25) beating Daniil Medvedev (11), appears as the single red line. Long lines are large ranking gaps, and the dropdown lets you see whether upsets become more common in later rounds.

Figure 08 · Bump Chart

Ranking Change Before vs After AO

Rankings only changed slightly in the four weeks after the tournament. Alcaraz and Sinner held the top two places, while players further down shifted by a few places depending on how deep they went. Blue lines show players who climbed, red lines those who fell, and grey lines those who stayed level.

In Figure 9, format shapes match length. Women’s best-of-three matches peak at around 18 games, while men’s best-of-five matches peak near 28 and have a second hump around 50, produced by long five-set matches. Move the slider through the rounds to see how these distributions change from the first round to the final.

Winning on serve separates winners from losers. In Figure 10 the men’s draw, winners take roughly 76-79% of their first-serve points against about 67-70% for losers, and the gap persists in every round. Shaded bands show the spread of results, and the dropdown lets you compare the women’s draw.

Section 5 · Multi Stat Profile

Figure 12 · Multi Bar Chart

No single statistic decides a semi-final. In several years, including 2016 and 2017, the losing semi-finalists hit more aces than the winners, so a big serve alone does not guarantee a place in the final. Use the stat buttons to compare aces, double faults, break points saved and rank.

Figure 13 · Sankey Diagram

Tournament Advancement Seeded vs Unseeded

Source: Tennis My Life

The draw narrows quickly for outsiders. Of the 128 men’s entrants, 96 were unseeded, but only 39 survived round 1 and just 9 reached round 3. None reached the round of 16, and seven of the top eight seeds reached the quarter-finals. The funnel ends with Carlos Alcaraz, the top seed, as champion.
